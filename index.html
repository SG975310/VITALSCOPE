Condition by condition

Prevention and management, in more depth

The habits above apply everywhere. Here's how each condition is specifically prevented, and broadly how doctors manage it once it's diagnosed.

Diabetes (Type 2)

Lower your risk

  • 150+ minutes a week of moderate activity (brisk walking, cycling) plus two strength sessions.
  • Swap refined grains and sugary drinks for whole grains, pulses, and vegetables — more fibre, steadier blood sugar.
  • If overweight, losing just 5–7% of body weight meaningfully cuts the odds of prediabetes progressing further.
  • Get fasting glucose or HbA1c checked every 1–3 years past age 35 — sooner with family history or a larger waist.

How it's typically managed

  • Diet, activity, and weight management stay the foundation even after diagnosis.
  • Metformin is usually the first medicine doctors prescribe.
  • Other classes — SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists, insulin — may be added depending on how blood sugar responds.
  • Regular eye, kidney, and foot checks catch complications early, before they're felt.

This is general education, not medical advice — any medicine or treatment decision should be made with a qualified doctor.

Heart Disease

Lower your risk

  • Not smoking is the single biggest lever — risk starts dropping within a year of quitting.
  • Keep cholesterol and blood pressure in check through diet (less saturated fat and salt, more fibre) and activity.
  • 150 minutes a week of moderate, or 75 minutes of vigorous, aerobic activity.
  • Limit alcohol and manage stress; get blood pressure and a lipid panel checked routinely.

How it's typically managed

  • Diet, activity, and quitting smoking remain central even after a diagnosis.
  • Statins are commonly prescribed to lower LDL cholesterol.
  • Blood pressure medicines (ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers) and, in higher-risk cases, low-dose aspirin may be used.
  • Significant blockages may need angioplasty/stenting or bypass surgery, often followed by cardiac rehab.

This is general education, not medical advice — any medicine or treatment decision should be made with a qualified doctor.

Hypertension

Lower your risk

  • Cap salt at around 5g (roughly one teaspoon) a day — ICMR's 2024 guidelines note average Indian intake runs almost double that.
  • Eat more potassium-rich fruit and vegetables, which helps offset sodium's effect on blood pressure.
  • Regular aerobic activity and a healthy weight — even modest weight loss lowers blood pressure.
  • Limit alcohol, don't smoke, and build in habits that actually reduce everyday stress.

How it's typically managed

  • A DASH-style diet (low sodium, high produce) plus exercise is first-line for milder cases.
  • ACE inhibitors/ARBs, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, and beta-blockers are common medicine classes — the choice depends on the person.
  • Home BP monitoring helps, since hypertension is usually symptomless until it isn't.
  • Periodic kidney and eye checks catch silent organ strain early.

This is general education, not medical advice — any medicine or treatment decision should be made with a qualified doctor.

Obesity

Lower your risk

  • Build meals around whole foods and watch portion sizes rather than following crash diets.
  • Mix aerobic and strength training — muscle itself raises resting metabolism.
  • Protect consistent, adequate sleep — short or irregular sleep disrupts the hunger hormones ghrelin and leptin.
  • Track BMI and waist size periodically, not just the number on the scale.

How it's typically managed

  • Structured, supervised diet-and-activity programs are first-line — not a single quick fix.
  • Some patients may be prescribed weight-management medicines (e.g. GLP-1 receptor agonists) under medical supervision.
  • Bariatric surgery is considered for select cases of severe obesity or serious related complications.
  • Long-term follow-up matters more than the initial result — regain without ongoing support is common.

This is general education, not medical advice — any medicine or treatment decision should be made with a qualified doctor.

Liver Disease (fatty-liver proxy)

Lower your risk

  • If overweight, losing just 5–10% of body weight measurably reduces fat stored in the liver.
  • Cut back on added sugar and refined carbs — especially sugary drinks, which drive fat storage in the liver directly.
  • Regular aerobic activity improves insulin sensitivity and helps clear fat from liver cells, even without weight loss.
  • Limit or avoid alcohol, and keep diabetes and cholesterol in check — both accelerate liver fat buildup.

How it's typically managed

  • Weight loss through diet and exercise is the main treatment — there's no widely approved medicine that reverses it on its own.
  • Doctors usually manage related conditions — diabetes, high cholesterol, obesity — together, since they drive the liver changes.
  • Ultrasound or elastography tracks liver fat and stiffness over time, to catch any progression toward inflammation or scarring.
  • Hepatitis A/B vaccination and avoiding unnecessary medications reduce added strain on an already stressed liver.

This is general education, not medical advice — any medicine or treatment decision should be made with a qualified doctor.

Chronic Kidney Disease

Lower your risk

  • Keep blood pressure and blood sugar well controlled — together they cause the large majority of CKD cases.
  • Avoid regular or long-term use of NSAID painkillers (like ibuprofen) unless a doctor specifically advises it.
  • Stay well hydrated, moderate salt and protein intake, and maintain a healthy weight.
  • Get a simple urine albumin and blood creatinine test periodically if you have diabetes, hypertension, or a family history.

How it's typically managed

  • Tight control of blood pressure and blood sugar slows progression significantly, even after some kidney damage has occurred.
  • ACE inhibitors or ARBs are commonly used since they help lower blood pressure and protect kidney function directly.
  • Dietary changes — lower sodium, moderated protein and potassium — are guided by a doctor or dietitian as kidney function changes.
  • In advanced stages, dialysis or transplant may eventually be needed — regular monitoring aims to delay or avoid that.

This is general education, not medical advice — any medicine or treatment decision should be made with a qualified doctor.

About this project

What's under the hood

The default Quick screening mode above runs entirely in your browser using a transparent, points-based heuristic built from established clinical risk factors — age, BMI, activity, family history, and similar — deliberately chosen so no lab test is required. BMI and waist-circumference cutoffs use the Asian-Indian-specific thresholds (overweight ≥23, obese ≥25 BMI; waist ≥90cm men / ≥80cm women) recommended by the 2009 Asian Indian consensus guidelines, rather than the higher WHO global cutoffs — since Asian Indians show elevated metabolic risk at lower BMI and waist measurements than the global standard assumes.

The diabetes, heart disease, hypertension, fatty liver, and kidney disease tabs also offer a Clinical mode for people who already have lab results in hand (glucose, cholesterol, ECG readings, blood/urine panels, and similar). This runs an actual trained logistic regression model — the same coefficients shown in the table below, evaluated in your browser via the identical math scikit-learn uses (standardize → weighted sum → sigmoid) — not a simulation of one.

Clinical mode pipeline
Your lab values→fill gaps with training-set median→standardize (z-score vs training mean/SD)→× learned coefficients + intercept→sigmoid→probability→risk band

Neither mode is a medical device, and neither diagnoses anything. Always confirm results with a qualified healthcare provider.

Reading these: precision is how often a positive prediction was actually correct; recall is how many true cases the model actually caught; accuracy is overall correctness; AUC summarizes how well the model ranks positive vs. negative cases across all thresholds. Obesity isn't included as a trained classifier — it's computed deterministically from BMI/waist ratio rather than being a diagnosis to predict, so it's scored with the same transparent points-based heuristic used elsewhere on quick mode. Note on the heart disease dataset: the commonly-mirrored copy of this dataset has a well-documented label inversion (its "target" column runs backwards from the intuitive "1 = disease present" reading) — verified here by checking that the raw label correlated with lower age and cholesterol before correcting it, and the metrics above are computed after that correction. Its chest-pain-type and thalassemia-test codings also follow the commonly-used reprocessed version, which differs slightly from the original UCI encoding. Note on the fatty liver classifier: there isn't a large public benchmark labeled specifically for fatty liver disease, so this one is trained on the Indian Liver Patient Dataset instead, which labels general liver-patient status (any hepatic disorder, not fatty liver specifically) from a standard liver function panel — a reasonable proxy given how tightly bilirubin, ALT/AST, and albumin track liver dysfunction generally, but a real fatty-liver-specific diagnosis (ultrasound or FibroScan) is more precise than this model can be. Note on the hypertension classifier: systolic and diastolic blood pressure are themselves the strongest predictors here, since they're clinically close to how hypertension is defined — this model is best read as combining a current BP reading with other risk factors into one probability, not as inferring hypertension from indirect signals alone.

Preprocessing: every clinical-mode feature is standardized (subtract the training-set mean, divide by its standard deviation) before the model’s coefficients are applied — identical to what scikit-learn’s StandardScaler does. Missing values: a blank field is filled with that feature’s training-set median (shown per-model in code), which can shift a prediction meaningfully for values far from typical — this is disclosed above each clinical form.
Confusion matrix, specificity, and class distribution: not yet published for these five models — accuracy/precision/recall/F1/AUC above come directly from the evaluation notebook, but per-class breakdowns aren’t reproduced in this demo yet. Treat that as an open item pending the full notebook writeup, not as evidence it doesn’t exist.
